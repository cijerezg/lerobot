# AWR as policy extraction: Park, Frans, Levine, Kumar 2024 (arXiv 2406.09329) and Park, Li, Levine 2025, Flow Q-Learning (arXiv 2502.02538)

Reading method: both papers were read in full from the arXiv v2 PDFs (main text, appendix, hyperparameter tables, per-task tables). The arXiv HTML renders were also fetched, but they duplicate LaTeX digits ("88 seeds", "22M steps", "0.000030.00003"), and an HTML summariser invented Figure 8 numbers for adroit tasks that the figure does not contain. Every number below was checked against the PDF. Numbers marked "approx., read from figure" come from plots or heat-map images and carry roughly ±2 (heat-map cells) or ±5 (bar charts) units of reading error. Numbers with no such mark come from printed tables or text.

Notation: both papers write the AWR weight as exp(alpha * (Q(s,a) - V(s))), where alpha is an *inverse* temperature (alpha = 1/beta in the reader's notation). alpha = 0 is plain BC. Neither paper states any advantage normalisation for AWR.

## Q1. Park et al. 2024: where AWR, DDPG+BC and SfBC are compared, the numbers, the explanation, the hyperparameters, the data-scaling matrices, and the test-time results

### Takeaway
Across 16 simulated settings (8 environments x 2 value learners, 8 seeds per cell), AWR averaged a median of about 0.88x the DDPG+BC score. The ratio ranged from 0.49x (exorl-walker) to 1.15x (pixel gc-roboverse with IQL), and DDPG+BC was at least as good as AWR in 15 of 16 settings (Table 1). The authors give three reasons for the gap. First, AWR is only mode-covering and cannot hill-climb Q. Second, exponential weights shrink the effective sample size and cause overfitting. Third, all weights are positive, so AWR can memorise every dataset action and drift back to plain BC. Their test-time fixes (OPEX, TTT) sit on top of an IQL+AWR policy and need grad_a Q(s,a). The gains appear only as bar charts.

### Cited Findings
**Paper identity (verified)**
- arXiv 2406.09329 is "Is Value Learning Really the Main Bottleneck in Offline RL?" by Seohong Park, Kevin Frans, Sergey Levine and Aviral Kumar (UC Berkeley and CMU). v1 is dated 13 Jun 2024 and v2 28 Oct 2024. The comments field reads "NeurIPS 2024". Title, authors and year match the assignment. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09329); [PDF v2 p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- There is one internal inconsistency in the run count. The v2 paper says the matrices are "aggregated from a total of 15,488 runs (8 seeds for each cell)" (§5.2). The project page says "7744 runs", which is exactly half. — [PDF v2 §5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2); [project page](https://seohong.me/projects/offrl-bottlenecks/)

**Objectives compared (§5.1.2, Eqs. 5–7)**
- AWR: max E_{s,a~D}[ e^{alpha(Q(s,a)-V(s))} log pi(a|s) ]. The paper describes it as follows: "AWR assigns larger weights to higher-advantage transitions when cloning behaviors, which makes the policy selectively copy only good actions from the dataset." — [PDF v2 §5.1.2 Eq. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- DDPG+BC: max E[ Q(s, mu^pi(s)) + alpha log pi(a|s) ], with mu^pi(s) = E_{a~pi}[a]. — [PDF v2 Eq. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- SfBC: pi(s) = argmax over a_1..a_N ~ pi^beta(.|s) of Q(s,a), where pi^beta is a learned BC policy. — [PDF v2 Eq. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- Value learners: SARSA (behavioural Q^beta), IQL (Q* via expectile) and CRL (goal-conditioned contrastive Q^beta). IQL and CRL are used for goal-conditioned tasks, IQL and SARSA for the rest. All of them are "decoupled": policy learning does not affect value learning. — [PDF v2 §5, §5.1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- The paper states that all three objectives share one principle: "maximizing values while being close to the behavioral policy, to avoid the exploitation of erroneous critic values." — [PDF v2 §5.1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)

**Environments (§5.1.3, App. C.1.1; all simulated)**
- gc-antmaze-large: goal-conditioned. 10M transitions collected with the D4RL noisy expert (sigma_data = 0.2). State observations.
- antmaze-large: D4RL antmaze-large-diverse-v2. State.
- d4rl-hopper and d4rl-walker2d: D4RL *-medium-v2, 1M transitions from "partially trained policies". State.
- exorl-walker and exorl-cheetah: ExORL walker-run and cheetah-run, 10M transitions from RND agents. The paper describes these as "purely unsupervised exploratory policies ... high suboptimality and high state-action diversity". State.
- kitchen: D4RL kitchen-mixed-v0 tasks with a newly collected 1M dataset from "a noisy, biased expert policy, where we add noises sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with a standard deviation of 0.2 in addition to a randomly initialized policy's actions to the expert policy's actions". State.
- gc-roboverse: the only pixel-based environment. Goal-conditioned robot-arm rearrangement from 48x48x3 images, 1M-transition dataset.
- Source: [PDF v2 §5.1.3, App. C.1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)

**Protocol (App. C.2, C.6)**
- Training runs 1M gradient steps (500K for gc-roboverse). Evaluation happens every 100K steps with 50 rollouts, and the reported score is the average of the last 3 evaluations over 8 seeds.
- The policy-extraction hyperparameter (alpha or N) is "individually tune[d] ... for each cell" and the best one is reported.
- Smaller datasets are nested prefixes of shuffled trajectories.
- Source: [PDF v2 App. C.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- Policies are Gaussian "without tanh squashing", with a fixed std of 1 for both AWR and DDPG+BC and a learnable std for SfBC. DDPG+BC actions are clipped to [-1, 1] in the Q term. With fixed std 1, the AWR log-likelihood reduces to a weighted MSE to the mean. — [PDF v2 App. C.6.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- The code base is jaxrl_minimal plus the HIQL implementation. The paper states no clipping of exp weights and no advantage normalisation. The project page links no code repository. — [PDF v2 App. C.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2); [project page](https://seohong.me/projects/offrl-bottlenecks/)

**Why DDPG+BC beats AWR (§5.4: three reasons, as stated)**
1. "AWR only has a mode-covering weighted behavioral cloning term, while DDPG+BC has both mode-seeking first-order value maximization and mode-covering behavioral cloning terms. As a result, actions learned by AWR always lie within the convex hull of dataset actions, whereas DDPG+BC can 'hillclimb' the learned value function, even allowing extrapolation to some degree". Figure 3 shows exorl-walker test-time actions: AWR actions are "relatively centered around the origin", while DDPG+BC actions "are more spread out". — [PDF v2 §5.4, Fig. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
2. Weighted BC "uses a much smaller number of effective samples ... especially when the temperature (α) is large ... a single transition with a weight of e^10 can dominate other transitions with smaller weights like e^2 ... AWR effectively uses only a fraction of datapoints for policy learning, being susceptible to overfitting."
   - Figure 4 (gc-antmaze-large, smallest 0.1M dataset, 8 seeds): AWR with alpha = 3.0 "causes severe overfitting". Its validation policy loss climbs to about 50+ while its training loss stays near 0–10 (approx., read from Figure 4).
   - AWR with alpha = 0 and DDPG+BC with alpha = 3.0 or 0.3 show overlapping train and validation curves.
   - The paper reports no ESS numbers; the argument is qualitative plus Figure 4.
   - Source: [PDF v2 §5.4, Fig. 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
3. "since the coefficient multiplying log π(a|s) in the AWR objective is always positive, AWR can increase the likelihood of *all* dataset actions, regardless of how optimal they are ... Under limited data coverage, and especially when the policy network is highly expressive and dataset states are unique (e.g., continuous control problems), AWR can in theory memorize all state-action pairs in the dataset, potentially reverting to *unweighted* behavioral cloning." — [PDF v2 §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- Takeaway box: "Do not use value-weighted behavior cloning (e.g., AWR); always use behavior-constrained policy gradient (e.g., DDPG+BC), regardless of the value learning objective." — [PDF v2 §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)

On antmaze, the best of deterministic evaluation and stochastic evaluation (fixed std 0.4) is reported for each method. — [PDF v2 App. C.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)

### Inferences
- Moving from IQL+AWR to SfBC with the same Q roughly matches OPEX on walker2d, exorl-walker and antmaze-large (approx. Figure 8). A large part of the test-time gain therefore comes from using Q(s,a) at action-selection time at all, not specifically from gradients. This is my reading of the figure; the authors do not break it down this way.
- AWR held up best (0.90–1.15x DDPG+BC) where the data came from a single noisy or partially trained policy (kitchen, D4RL-medium) and on the only pixel task (roboverse-IQL). It collapsed (about 0.5x) on unsupervised exploratory data (exorl). Teleop demos plus autonomous rollouts with failures look more like the first group than like exorl.
- AWR, DDPG+BC and SfBC as defined here, and OPEX and TTT, all use Q(s,a). The reader's critic is V(s) only, with A = r + gamma*V(s') - V(s). That is a one-sample TD estimate of Q - V, and neither paper tests it. Of the families studied, only AWR can run on a V-only critic without adding an action-conditioned (chunk-conditioned) Q.

### Gaps
- Park 2024 does not state whether AWR weights were clipped (e.g., at 100). No code is linked. The only defaults given are "IQL / goal-conditioned IQL / CRL" hyperparameters.
- There are no numeric OPEX/TTT results (bar chart only), and there are no per-cell tables for Figures 1, 2, 7 and 11 (heat-map images only; low-res cells are partly illegible).
- The 15,488 vs 7,744 run-count discrepancy between the paper (v2) and the project page is unexplained.

## Q2. Park et al. 2024: robustness to value errors, drift to BC at high temperature, sensitivity to data quality

### Takeaway
The paper never isolates robustness to value errors. The only related evidence is that DDPG+BC's advantage persists even with poor, low-data value functions (§5.2), so there is no evidence here that AWR is the safer choice with a bad critic. Drift toward BC is argued in the opposite direction from the usual intuition. It comes at *low* alpha (alpha = 0 is BC), and at any alpha under limited coverage with an expressive network through memorisation (§5.4). High alpha instead causes effective-sample collapse and overfitting (Figure 4). On data quality, AWR's relative deficit is largest on exploratory, highly suboptimal data (exorl) and smallest on noisy-expert or partially trained data (kitchen, D4RL-medium).

### Cited Findings
- The extractors all stay close to the behaviour policy "to avoid the exploitation of erroneous critic values". This is stated as motivation, not measured per method. — [PDF v2 §5.1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- "Such a performance gap between policy extraction algorithms exists even when the value function is far from perfect, as can be seen in the low-data regimes in gc-antmaze-large and kitchen." — [PDF v2 §5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- "AWR is always policy-bounded regardless of the BC strength α". More value data, and so a better value function, mostly does not help AWR, whereas DDPG+BC with small alpha is value-bounded. — [PDF v2 §5.3, Fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- "α = 0 corresponds to BC in AWR". — [PDF v2 §5.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- Memorisation pathology: "AWR can in theory memorize all state-action pairs in the dataset, potentially reverting to unweighted behavioral cloning". It is tied to "limited data coverage", a "highly expressive" policy network and "unique" dataset states. — [PDF v2 §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- High alpha: "a much smaller number of effective samples ... especially when the temperature (α) is large", with severe overfitting at alpha = 3 on 0.1M data (Figure 4). exorl-walker does not become strong even at alpha = 100. — [PDF v2 §5.3–5.4, Figs. 2 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- Data quality, AWR vs DDPG+BC (Table 1):
  - exorl (RND exploration, "high suboptimality"): 99 vs 191 and 94 vs 193 (walker); 71 vs 101 and 78 vs 131 (cheetah).
  - kitchen (noisy, biased expert: expert actions plus N(0, 0.2) noise plus random-policy actions): 80 vs 86 and 79 vs 83.
  - D4RL-medium (partially trained policy): 53 vs 52 and 56 vs 61 (hopper); 73 vs 81 and 79 vs 84 (walker2d).
  - Source: [PDF v2 Table 1, App. C.1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- Noisier, higher-coverage data helps offline RL in general, but "AWR fails to fully leverage the value function" in low-data gc-antmaze-large. — [PDF v2 §6.3, Fig. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)

### Inferences
- The reader's standardise-then-clip scheme (clip ±3, beta = 1) bounds the max/min weight ratio at e^6 ≈ 403. The "e^10 vs e^2" domination example in §5.4 corresponds to an 8-unit spread in alpha*A, which the reader's clip rules out.
- If Â were ~N(0,1) and unclipped, w = exp(Â/beta) would give an ESS fraction (E w)^2/E[w^2] = exp(-1/beta^2) ≈ 0.37 at beta = 1, or about 47 effective rows out of 128. This is my analytic calculation, not a result from either paper, and clipping at ±3 changes it only slightly. Neither paper tested normalised or clipped AWR, so how the Figure 4 overfitting transfers to the reader's scheme is open.
- The memorisation argument fits a VLA closely: a large expressive network and unique image states. It predicts that, given enough epochs, AWR's weighting on a fixed offline set fades toward BC. The reader's actor loss would therefore mostly change which samples dominate early training, not what training converges to. This follows from the §5.4 argument and was not measured for large models.

### Gaps
- No experiment injects value noise or compares AWR and DDPG+BC under controlled critic error.
- There is no experiment on mixed demo-plus-failure data specifically. The closest proxies are kitchen's noisy expert and Figure 7's noise sweep.
- AWR is not evaluated with advantage normalisation, weight clipping, or weight-floor mixing (the reader's lam).

## Q3. Flow Q-Learning (FQL): what FAWAC is exactly, its numbers against FQL, IFQL, FBRAC and the BC-like baselines, and why the authors think it is weak

### Takeaway
FAWAC is literally the reader's loss form: per-sample flow-matching loss multiplied by exp(alpha*(Q - V)) (Eq. 10). It was tuned over alpha in {0.3, 1, 3, 10} per environment, and the paper states no clipping or normalisation. Averaged over 50 state-based OGBench tasks it scored 16, against 29 (FBRAC), 30 (IFQL) and 44 (FQL) (Figure 5). It was usually *below* Gaussian AWR (IQL): lower in 8 of 10 state-based categories (higher only on antsoccer 12 vs 8 and scene 30 vs 28), and also lower on D4RL antmaze (44 vs 57) and adroit (48 vs 53). It matched Gaussian BC on D4RL adroit (48 vs 48). It was not run on pixel tasks or offline-to-online. The authors blame "the small number of effective samples and limited expressivity" (§4.1).

### Cited Findings
**Paper identity (verified)**
- arXiv 2502.02538 is "Flow Q-Learning" by Seohong Park, Qiyang Li and Sergey Levine (UC Berkeley). v1 is dated 4 Feb 2025 and v2 25 May 2025, with "ICML 2025" (PMLR 267). Title, authors and year match the assignment. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02538); [PDF v2 p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)

**FAWAC definition (§4.1 Eq. 10, §5.1, App. E.2)**
- Objective: max_theta E_{s,a~D}[ e^{alpha(Q(s,a) - V(s))} L_Flow(theta) ], where "α is an inverse temperature hyperparameter". The paper writes max, but L_Flow is a loss, so this is the weighted *minimisation* of flow-matching error. — [PDF v2 §4.1 Eq. 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)
- The flow-matching loss is L_Flow = E ||v_theta(t, s, x^t) - (x^1 - x^0)||^2, with a linear path and t ~ Unif[0,1] (Eq. 5). The weight multiplies this per-sample loss. — [PDF v2 §2 Eq. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)
- "Flow advantage-weighted actor-critic (FAWAC) is a flow variant of AWAC (Nair et al., 2020), which uses AWR (Equation (10)) as the policy learning objective, conceptually similar to QIPO". "FAWAC and FBRAC are different from our method (FQL) *only* by their policy extraction strategies while sharing the exact same architectures and implementations". — [PDF v2 §5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)
- The appendix calls it "FAWR". FAWAC and FBRAC use "the same default [hyperparameters] as FQL". "For the inverse temperature α in FAWR (Equation (10)), we consider {0.3, 1, 3, 10}." — [PDF v2 App. E.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)

FAWAC/FBRAC code is **not** in the official repo. There is no normalisation, and the Gaussian is constant-std. — [agents/iql.py](https://github.com/seohongpark/fql/blob/master/agents/iql.py)

**Other baselines (§4.1, §5.1, App. E.2)**
- FBRAC: the flow version of Diffusion-QL, i.e. Q loss plus alpha * L_Flow with backprop through the ODE steps (Eq. 6). alpha is swept over {1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300} (OGBench, D4RL antmaze) or {1000, ..., 30000} (adroit).
- IFQL: the flow version of IDQL, with rejection sampling from a fixed BC flow (argmax_i Q(s, a_i)) and N in {32, 64, 128}, on top of IQL value learning.
- FQL: a one-step policy mu_omega trained with -Q + alpha * ||mu_omega(s,z) - mu_theta(s,z)||^2, distilling from a BC flow and needing no BPTT (Eq. 9). alpha is swept over {3, 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000} (6 values) for OGBench and D4RL antmaze.
- Gaussian IQL: AWR inverse temperatures {0.3, 1, 3, 10}. Smaller [256×4] MLPs were used for state-based OGBench manipulation because "IQL tends to overfit".
- BC: Gaussian with unit std.
- Source: [PDF v2 §3, §4.1, App. B, App. E.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)
- Tuning protocol: "we *individually* tune hyperparameters of the baselines with similar amounts of training budget". For OGBench, hyperparameters are tuned on the default task of each environment and reused for the other four. Scores average the last three evaluations (800K/900K/1M for state tasks, 300K/400K/500K for pixel tasks) with no best-epoch selection. There are 8 seeds for state tasks and 4 for pixel tasks, and each evaluation uses 50 episodes. — [PDF v2 §5.1, App. B, App. E.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)

### Inferences
- Two results imply that switching from a Gaussian to an expressive flow policy did not rescue AWR. On the same benchmark, FAWAC < IQL(Gaussian AWR) in 8 of 10 state OGBench categories, and 44 vs 57 on D4RL antmaze. This is confounded, though. IQL used an expectile V, clipped weights (at 100 in the released code) and smaller MLPs chosen against overfitting, while FAWAC used FQL's value defaults with an unspecified V.
- FAWAC's best category was cube-single (81, vs 5 for Gaussian BC). Its OGBench manipulation averages sit far below FQL on cube-double (5 vs 29), puzzle (6/1 vs 30/17) and scene (30 vs 56). On adroit (human demos, cloned and expert data; 24-D actions) FAWAC ≈ BC ≈ FQL. The reader's teleop-plus-rollout data is closer in spirit to adroit and cube-single than to OGBench "play" and stitching tasks.

### Gaps
- The exact FAWAC implementation is unknown: how V(s) is computed, any clipping or normalisation, and whether weights are per sample or per (sample, t, noise). There is no public code.
- There are no FAWAC results on pixel or offline-to-online tasks, and no alpha-sensitivity curves for FAWAC (Figures 7 and 10 ablate only FQL).
- Flow BC (FAWAC with alpha → 0) is not reported. The BC column is Gaussian BC, so the gain of flow-AWR over flow-BC cannot be isolated.

## Q4. Both papers: real-robot results, action chunking, high-dimensional actions

### Takeaway
Neither paper has real-robot experiments, action chunking, or a VLA-scale model. All results are simulated MLP (or small CNN) policies. Pixel observations appear only in gc-roboverse (48×48, Park 2024) and 5 OGBench visual tasks (64×64, FQL). The highest action dimensionality discussed is adroit's 24-D in FQL.

## Q5. Both papers: failure modes and diagnostics (effective sample size, weight spread, drift to BC, hyperparameter sensitivity)

### Takeaway
The only quantitative AWR diagnostic reported is Figure 4 in Park 2024: the training vs validation policy-loss gap, which shows overfitting at alpha = 3 on 0.1M data. Neither paper reports ESS values, weight histograms, max-weight share or fraction clipped. Hyperparameter sensitivity for AWR is visible only in Park 2024's per-alpha data-scaling matrices (Figures 2 and 11).

### Cited Findings
- Park 2024 diagnostics:
  - The overfitting gap in Figure 4.
  - Colour-gradient reading of the data-scaling matrices, where policy-bounded means the value function is under-used (§5.2).
  - The action spread in Figure 3.
  - Train, validation and evaluation MSE against an expert policy, with a 95/5 trajectory split (§6.1, App. C.3). Evaluation MSE was "the most predictive of the performance" (§6.2).

- Park 2024 on sensitivity: the best alpha differs by environment and by data size (the grids in Table 3; per-cell tuning, App. C.2). - FQL: the paper names the BC coefficient as FQL's most important hyperparameter (Figure 7, Figure 10a). It is "tuned for each environment based on the suboptimality of the dataset". The paper recommends Q normalisation for new tasks, with alpha tuned from {0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10}. ### Inferences
- A Figure 4-style check fits the reader's setup directly: compare per-sample flow loss on a held-out episode split under w vs w = 1. If held-out loss rises while training loss falls, that is the Park overfitting signature. Logging per-batch ESS (Σw)^2/Σw^2 and the share of weight held by the top 5% of rows would quantify the "few effective samples" mechanism that both papers cite but neither measures. ## Q6. Critique: where the comparisons are unfair or limited

### Takeaway
Within each paper the comparisons are reasonably even-handed: equal-size alpha grids per method in Park 2024, and the same code base for the flow variants in FQL. They are still narrow. The AWR tested is vanilla: raw advantages, no normalisation, and clipping unstated or at 100. The environments are simulated, mostly state-based and often sparse-reward goal-reaching. All evidence is on MLP-scale policies. FQL's FAWAC also got a smaller alpha grid than FQL, was tuned only on one task per environment, and was dropped from the pixel and fine-tuning evaluations.

### Cited Findings
- Park 2024 tuning:
  - Each extractor gets a 4-value grid (3 for SfBC), tuned *per matrix cell* on evaluation return (App. C.2, Table 3). This is online model selection, which is optimistic for every method.
  - The AWR grid includes alpha = 0 (BC), so only 3 non-trivial temperatures are tried.
  - AWR uses a fixed-std-1 unimodal Gaussian (weighted MSE) (App. C.6.1).
  - 8 seeds, reported as mean ± std (§5.2).
  - Source: [PDF v2 App. C.2, C.6.1, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- Park 2024 scope:
  - 7 of 8 analysis environments are state-based. The single pixel one (48×48 roboverse) is where AWR+IQL scored best (23 vs 20 vs 14).
  - antmaze-large with SARSA is 0 for all methods and carries no information.
  - OPEX and TTT are shown only as bars, and only on top of IQL+AWR.
  - Source: [PDF v2 Table 1, Fig. 8, App. C.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09329v2)
- FQL tuning:
  - FAWAC alpha grid {0.3, 1, 3, 10} (4 values) vs FQL alpha {3, ..., 1000} (6 values) on OGBench.
  - IFQL N has 3 values; FBRAC alpha has 6.
  - OGBench hyperparameters are tuned on the default task and transferred to 4 other tasks.
  - Several D4RL baseline numbers are copied from prior work rather than rerun.
  - Source: [PDF v2 App. E.2, Table 2 caption, App. D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)
- FQL scope:
  - FAWAC is absent from the visual tasks ("we selectively benchmark 5 methods that achieve strong performance on state-based OGBench tasks") and from offline-to-online.
  - Pixel tasks use only task1 and 4 seeds.
  - The OGBench datasets are "play"/"navigate" data collected "performing *random* tasks" and "require a high degree of 'stitching'", with semi-sparse rewards (minus the number of remaining subtasks).
  - Source: [PDF v2 Table 2 footnote, App. E.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2)
- FAWAC's value-learning path differs from the Gaussian IQL baseline's, and the paper leaves it unspecified. The released Gaussian IQL clips weights at 100, while FAWAC's clipping is unknown. — [PDF v2 App. E.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02538v2); [agents/iql.py](https://github.com/seohongpark/fql/blob/master/agents/iql.py)

### Inferences
- Four points limit how far these results transfer to a real-robot flow VLA:
  1. Both papers' headline advice, "use DDPG+BC" and "use one-step reparameterised guidance", assumes a differentiable Q(s,a). The reader's V-only, one-step-TD advantage setup has no counterpart in either paper.
  2. Both papers train from scratch on 0.1M–10M transitions with MLPs. A pretrained VLA fine-tuned on a small real dataset sits in Park's "limited coverage, highly expressive network, unique states" regime (§5.4), where the memorise-back-to-BC argument is strongest.
  3. The reader's normalisation, clip and lam differ from every AWR variant tested. The ESS and domination arguments may be weaker at beta = 1 with clip ±3 than at the paper's alpha = 3–100 on raw advantages.
  4. Sparse-reward goal-conditioned mazes and exploratory ExORL data are where AWR's deficit is largest, and they are furthest from teleop-plus-rollout data. Settings with a noisy expert or partially trained policy (kitchen, D4RL-medium, adroit) put AWR at about 0.9–1.0x of the best alternative. A realistic expectation from this evidence is AWR ≈ BC plus a modest reweighting gain, not large policy improvement.

### Gaps
- Neither paper tests AWR under a matched, larger tuning budget, with advantage normalisation, or with an expressive policy *and* a well-specified V. So "AWR is weak" is established for vanilla AWR on these benchmarks, not for the reader's variant.
